If you're stuck at Band 6 in Speaking, your English is almost certainly not the problem. You can hold a conversation. You understand the questions. You can talk about your job, your hometown and your hobbies without much trouble.

That's exactly what Band 6 means. The examiner heard someone who can communicate. What they didn't hear was the specific set of things Band 7 requires, and none of them are about knowing more English.

## How the Speaking test is actually marked

The examiner scores you on four criteria, each worth a quarter of your Speaking band:

1. **Fluency and Coherence**: how long you can keep going, and whether your ideas connect logically
2. **Lexical Resource**: the range and precision of your vocabulary
3. **Grammatical Range and Accuracy**: how many kinds of sentence you use, and how often they're correct
4. **Pronunciation**: how easy you are to understand, including stress, rhythm and intonation

Your final Speaking band is the average of the four. That matters, because it means you don't need to be perfect at everything. You need to stop losing marks in the same predictable places.

## Where Band 6 speakers lose their marks

### 1. Answers that stop too soon

This is the biggest one. The examiner asks a question, you give a sensible answer, and then you stop.

> **Examiner:** Do you think people today rely too much on technology?
>
> **Candidate:** Yes, I think so. People use their phones all the time.

Nothing is wrong with that answer. It's grammatically correct and it answers the question. But it gives the examiner almost nothing to score. At Band 7, the public descriptors talk about speaking *at length* without noticeable effort. A two-sentence answer can't demonstrate that, however good your English is.

### 2. Linking words that sound memorised

Many candidates know they need to connect ideas, so they learn a list: *firstly, moreover, furthermore, in addition, on the other hand*. Then they use them like bolts holding sentences together.

Band 6 descriptors mention using connectives "not always appropriately". Band 7 asks for them to be used *with some flexibility*. In speech, that usually means fewer formal linkers and more natural ones: *the thing is*, *that's partly because*, *mind you*, *which is why*.

### 3. Safe vocabulary

Band 6 vocabulary gets the job done. Band 7 vocabulary is more precise and includes some less common words and collocations, used naturally.

The trap is trying to fix this with memorised "advanced" phrases. Examiners hear *it is a double-edged sword* hundreds of times a week. A better approach is replacing your most overused words with precise ones:

| Instead of | Try |
| --- | --- |
| very important | essential, crucial, a priority |
| a lot of people | a growing number of people, most people I know |
| good / bad | worthwhile, rewarding / harmful, counterproductive |
| I think | I'd say, my sense is, as far as I can tell |

### 4. Grammar that plays it safe

At Band 6, you use a mix of simple and complex sentences, but the complex ones often contain errors, so many candidates avoid them altogether. At Band 7, you need a range of complex structures and a good number of error-free sentences.

You don't need exotic grammar. Conditionals, relative clauses and a few comparisons used correctly will do far more for you than one ambitious inversion that falls apart halfway.

## What a Band 7 answer sounds like

Here's the same question again, answered with the gaps closed:

> **Candidate:** To some extent, yes. I think the problem isn't using technology, it's that a lot of us have stopped doing things without it. Take navigation: ten years ago most people could find their way around their own city, whereas now plenty of people I know would genuinely be lost without Google Maps. So I'd say we rely on it more than we realise, and that only becomes obvious when the battery dies.

Look at what changed:

- **It keeps going** without sounding rehearsed.
- **It makes a point, explains it, and gives an example.** This is the PEE structure I teach every student, and you can [read how it works here](/blog/pee-method-ielts).
- **The linking is natural:** *take navigation*, *whereas*, *so I'd say*.
- **There's a range of grammar:** a comparison across time, a conditional idea, a relative clause.
- **The vocabulary is precise** without being showy: *genuinely*, *rely on it more than we realise*.

None of the words in that answer are difficult. A Band 6 speaker knows all of them. The difference is technique.

## How to start closing the gap this week

1. **Record yourself answering three Part 3 questions.** Time each answer. If most are under 20 seconds, development is your first job.
2. **Add one sentence of explanation and one example to every answer.** It will feel unnatural for a few days, then it won't.
3. **Pick your three most overused words** and find two precise replacements for each.
4. **Stop memorising idioms.** Use the time to practise speaking out loud instead.

If you've already sat the test once or twice and the number won't move, that usually means you need someone to tell you exactly which criterion is costing you marks. That's what the trial lesson is for.
