The most common thing my Band 6 students tell me is some version of this: *"I know what I want to say, but I run out after one sentence."*

They answer the question, then stop. Then they panic, because they can feel the silence. In Writing it shows up as a different problem: paragraphs that list three ideas in three sentences and develop none of them.

The fix is the same in both cases. It's called PEE.

## What PEE stands for

- **Point**: answer the question directly, in one sentence.
- **Explain**: say *why*. What's the reason behind your point?
- **Example**: make it concrete. A real situation, something you've seen, a specific case.

That's it. It's simple on purpose. Under exam pressure you won't remember a complicated system, but you will remember three letters.

## PEE in Speaking Part 3

Part 3 is where most Band 6 candidates get stuck, because the questions are abstract and there's no obvious "story" to tell.

> **Examiner:** Why do some people prefer to work from home?

**Without PEE:**

> I think because it's more comfortable and they don't have to travel.

**With PEE:**

> **(Point)** Mainly, I think it's about time. **(Explain)** Commuting can eat up an hour or two a day, and when you work from home you get that time back, which you can spend on family or just resting. **(Example)** My cousin used to spend nearly three hours a day on trains, and since she started working remotely she says she finally has evenings again.

The second answer is roughly four times longer, and every extra sentence earns marks. It shows Fluency and Coherence, because the ideas connect logically. It creates natural space for vocabulary (*eat up*, *commuting*, *remotely*) and for a range of grammar.

Notice that it didn't need any clever words. It just needed a structure to follow.

### Going further: PEE twice

Once PEE becomes automatic, you can extend an answer by adding a second point, often a contrast:

> ...she finally has evenings again. **(Point 2)** That said, I don't think it suits everyone. **(Explain)** Some people really need the separation between home and work, otherwise they never switch off...

This is how Band 7 speakers sound fluent. They aren't talking faster. They always know what comes next.

## PEE in Writing Task 2

In Writing, PEE becomes the backbone of each body paragraph. The marker is looking for a clear position and ideas that are *extended and supported*, not just listed.

**A Band 6-style paragraph:**

> Working from home has many advantages. Firstly, people save time. Secondly, they can save money. Moreover, they can have a better work-life balance.

Three ideas, none developed. It reads like a list with linking words glued on.

**A PEE paragraph:**

> **(Point)** The most significant advantage of remote work is the time it returns to employees. **(Explain)** Daily commuting in large cities can take up to two hours, time which is neither productive for the company nor restful for the worker. When this is removed, employees are able to start the day less fatigued and to use the recovered hours for family, exercise or further study. **(Example)** Many firms that adopted remote working during the pandemic reported that staff were reluctant to return to the office full time, largely for this reason.

One idea, fully developed. This is what "relevant, extended and supported ideas" looks like in practice.

## Common mistakes with PEE

- **Making the explanation repeat the point.** "Working from home saves time *because you don't waste time*" isn't an explanation. Ask yourself *why does that matter?*
- **Inventing statistics in Writing.** You don't need exact numbers, and made-up ones can undermine your argument. "Many", "a significant number" and a general real-world example are safer.
- **Forcing it into Part 1.** Part 1 questions are short and personal. A point plus one extra detail is enough there. Save the full PEE for Part 3 and for your essays.
- **Saying the labels out loud.** Obvious, but it happens. The structure should be invisible to the examiner.

## How to practise it

1. Take five Part 3 questions. Answer each one out loud using P, E and E, and record yourself.
2. Listen back. Did each part actually do its job, or did the explanation just repeat the point?
3. Take your last Task 2 essay and rewrite one body paragraph so it develops a single idea using PEE.
